# GeoCrimp #041 — Caption

Somewhere in the rock there is a clay layer a few centimetres thick that ends an era.

About 66 million years ago an asteroid hit what is now the Yucatán and left the Chicxulub crater. The material it vaporised spread around the planet and settled out as a single thin layer — and that layer carries iridium. Iridium is rare in Earth's crust, because it went down into the core with the iron early on. It is far more common in asteroids. Finding it concentrated in one worldwide horizon is what turned the impact from a guess into a case, and the layer sits exactly at the Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary.

Fun fact: where the boundary is exposed you can literally put a finger on it. Below the layer, one fossil world. Above it, a different one — and the layer itself is thin enough to cover with a hand.

Source: Alvarez et al. (1980) and Smit & Hertogen (1980) — iridium anomaly at the K-Pg boundary · 'Globally distributed iridium layer preserved within the Chicxulub impact structure', Science Advances (2021).
