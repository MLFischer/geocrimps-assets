# GeoCrimp #054 — Caption

Your crag is eroding roughly eighteen times slower than the valley below it.

Cosmic rays produce beryllium-10 in quartz within the top few metres of the ground, so its concentration in a rock surface records how long that surface has been sitting there. Compile enough of those measurements and you get a global picture of how fast the land actually wears down. Portenga and Bierman did that with 1,599 measurements: outcrops erode at an average of about 12 metres per million years, with a median of 5.4. Sampled drainage basins average about 218 metres per million years, median 54.

Twelve metres per million years is roughly a centimetre per thousand years. On the timescale of a climbing life, the wall is not going anywhere.

Fun fact: rock type shows up in the numbers. Sedimentary outcrops erode fastest at about 20 metres per million years, metamorphic about 11, igneous about 8.7 — the last two statistically similar to each other. And the authors are candid that outcrop rates are the harder half of the problem: only about a third of the variation is explained, and they suggest fracture density, joint spacing, structure and rock strength are the missing terms.

Source: Portenga & Bierman (2011), Understanding Earth's eroding surface with 10Be, GSA Today 21(8):4–10.
