# GeoCrimp #045 — Caption

The colour of the wall usually isn't the rock.

Those black streaks, brown patches and orange tapestries on a desert cliff are coatings — thin skins that sit on the surface and are compositionally different from the bedrock underneath. They are built from windblown clay dust, from water running down the face, and from microorganisms living on the rock. Rock varnish, the classic dark one, is mostly clay minerals bound to the surface by iron and manganese oxides. More manganese, blacker the varnish; more iron, redder and browner. What is still not settled is how the manganese gets concentrated in the first place — the mechanisms are not completely understood, and both chemical and microbial pathways are on the table.

Fun fact: this is why petroglyphs work. Chipping through a varnished surface exposes the pale rock beneath, and the contrast is still there centuries later — the coating grows on the order of micrometres per thousand years. On a varnished wall, a fresh break stays a different colour for a very long time.

Source: Desert Varnish, National Park Service (Geologic Resources) · Dorn (2022), Terrestrial Rock Coatings, in Treatise on Geomorphology, 2nd ed.
