# GeoCrimp #052 — Caption

There is a field test that tells sandstone from quartzite, and it is about where the rock breaks.

Sandstone is grains plus whatever glues them together, so it fails along the boundaries — break a piece and you get a rough, sandy surface of intact grains. Quartzite has been through metamorphism, and metamorphism recrystallises the sand grains and the silica cement together into one interlocking network. Geology.com puts the consequence bluntly: it is so tough that it breaks through the quartz grains rather than breaking along the boundaries between them. That single difference is what separates true quartzite from sandstone.

The pore space goes in the same process. Quartzite is dense, hard — about seven on the Mohs scale — and chemically about as unreactive as a rock gets.

Fun fact: this is why quartzite ends up on the skyline. When a mountain range is worn down, the less resistant rock goes first and the quartzite is left standing, which is why it is so often the rock at the crest of a range, or the boulder field on its flanks. It is also a poor soil-former — its weathering debris is just quartz — so it tends to sit as bare rock with little or no soil on it.

Honest note: geologists have used the word two ways. Some older work calls any very quartz-rich sedimentary rock a quartzite; today most reserve it for the metamorphic rock, and the transition between the two is gradual rather than sharp.

Source: King, Quartzite — The metamorphic rock composed almost entirely of quartz, Geology.com.
