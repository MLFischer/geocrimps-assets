# GeoCrimp #030 — Caption

Slate doesn't split along its layers. It splits along its memory of being squeezed.

Slate starts as mud, settling in flat layers on a sea floor. Then a mountain-building event squeezes it from the sides. Under that stress the tiny clay and mica flakes rotate and regrow until they all lie at right angles to the direction of shortening. That fabric is called slaty cleavage, and the rock now splits along it — usually at a clear angle to the original bedding. It's why slate makes roof tiles. It's also why the quarried walls above Llanberis climb the way they do: enormous clean planes, and very little to hold on to.

Fun fact: you can often read both grains in one block — a faint colour banding left from the original bedding, crossed at an angle by the shiny surface the rock actually breaks on.

Source: Waldron & Snyder, Geological Structures — A Practical Introduction (fabrics, cleavage) · slaty cleavage as preferred orientation of phyllosilicates perpendicular to maximum shortening.
