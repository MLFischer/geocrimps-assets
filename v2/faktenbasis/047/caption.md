# GeoCrimp #047 — Caption

Those sweeping diagonal lines in a sandstone wall are dunes. Actual dunes.

Wind pushes sand up the gentle windward side of a dune and drops it over the crest. On the steep downwind face the sand piles up until it fails and slides — a small avalanche — and each slide leaves its own inclined layer. Stack thousands of them and the whole lee face becomes a set of slanted beds. Then the dune migrates, the next one planes off its top, and the record is a series of truncated packages of tilted layers: cross-bedding. And because those layers were built by sand sliding downwind, the direction they lean is a compass needle pointing the way the wind blew.

Fun fact: read the Navajo Sandstone that way and you get a sand sea deposited in the Early Jurassic, roughly 190 million years ago, covering an estimated 400,000 km² — with cross-bed orientations indicating dominant winds from the north-northwest. Dating of detrital zircons suggests at least some of that sand started in the ancient Appalachians and crossed the continent by river before the desert wind ever touched it.

Source: Navajo Sandstone, Plate Tectonic Stories, The Geological Society of London · Rahl et al. (2003), Geology — detrital zircon provenance.
