# GeoCrimp #043 — Caption

That wet streak on the wall isn't random. It's a horizon.

Rain soaks into porous rock and keeps sinking, as long as there is pore space to sink through. Where it reaches a layer water can barely pass — mudstone, marl, shale — it stops going down and starts going sideways, piling up above that layer as perched groundwater. Wherever that boundary meets the cliff face, the water comes back out. Because the boundary is a bed and beds run more or less level, the seeps line up: springs at roughly the same height, along one line. Geologists call it a spring line. Climbers call it the section that never dries.

Fun fact: Zion's Weeping Rock works exactly this way. Water sinks through the thick, porous Navajo Sandstone and reaches the tighter mudstones and siltstones of the Kayenta Formation underneath — and comes out of the wall at that contact, feeding the hanging gardens.

Source: perched aquifers and spring discharge — The Groundwater Project, Hydrogeologic Properties of Earth Materials · Ground-Water Discharge from the Navajo Sandstone, National Park Service technical report.
