# GeoCrimp #057 — Caption

Some walls are upside down, and the rock will tell you — if you ask it more than once.

Sedimentary beds are not symmetrical. Many of them carry structures that look different right-side-up than they do upside-down, and geologists call these way-up structures, or geopetal structures. They are how you work out the younging direction: the direction in which the strata get younger. In a folded or overturned wall, that is not the same as "upwards".

The most useful one is graded bedding. In normal graded bedding the coarse material sits at the bottom of the bed and fines gradually towards the top. So in most cases you can find the coarsest part, head towards the finest part, and be confident the strata get younger in that direction. Cross-beds work too: the laminae are tangential at their base and truncated at the top.

Here is the part that matters. There are exceptions. Reverse grading is real — debris flows and lahars can leave coarse material at the top. Way-up structures in the field are frequently ambiguous. The advice from the textbook is to be cautious about drawing big conclusions from a single indicator, to look for as many examples as possible, and to check whether they agree with each other.

Fun fact: metamorphism erases the evidence. Low-grade rocks can keep primary structures from their protolith, but higher-grade metamorphic rocks are recrystallised so thoroughly that any original way-up structure is destroyed. A gneiss will not tell you which way was up.

Source: Way-up structures, in Historical Geology (Callan Bentley et al., OpenGeology).
