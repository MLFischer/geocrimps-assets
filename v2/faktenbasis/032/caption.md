# GeoCrimp #032 — Caption

Lichen on a hold isn't just dirt. It's a slow attack on the rock — and sometimes its protection.

A lichen is a fungus farming algae. The fungal threads work their way down between mineral grains and prise them apart, and the whole body swells and shrinks with every wetting and drying, loosening grains mechanically. Chemically, lichens release organic acids — above all oxalic acid — which dissolve minerals and pull metal ions out of them. But the same cover also shelters the rock from rain and temperature swings, and the calcium oxalate crust that forms at the contact is barely soluble. The literature describes both: biodeterioration and bioprotection. Which one wins depends on the rock and the climate.

Fun fact: because lichens grow slowly and steadily, their size has been used to estimate how long a rock surface has been exposed — lichenometry. How precise that is gets argued about, but the principle holds: the lichen on your project has been there a while.

Source: Chen, Blume & Beyer (2000), 'Weathering of rocks induced by lichen colonization — a review', Catena · Adamo & Violante (2000), Applied Clay Science · studies on lichen bioprotection of calcareous surfaces.
