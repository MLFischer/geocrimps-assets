# GeoCrimp #044 — Caption

The coldest nights aren't the ones that break your crag.

Everyone knows the line: water freezes, expands by about 9 %, and levers the rock apart. It is real physics — but the conditions it needs are strict. The pores have to be almost completely full, around 91 % saturated, and the freeze has to be fast. On a real rock wall that combination is rare.

The other route is quieter. Even in frozen rock, a film of unfrozen water only nanometres thick clings to the walls of pores and cracks. Water creeps along that film towards the ice already sitting there, and the ice keeps growing and pushing outwards. This is ice segregation, and it is most effective inside a narrow band of temperature — the frost cracking window — a few degrees below zero. Deeper cold isn't automatically worse: it slows the water down that feeds the ice.

Fun fact: the window isn't one number. It has been measured at about −3 to −6 °C in a weak sandstone, and models put it as low as −15 to −5 °C for strong rock. Different crags have different damage temperatures.

Honest caveat: whether many short freeze–thaw swings or long sustained freezes do more damage is still open — a 2024 laboratory study on Dachstein limestone found repeated cycles more damaging, and notes this diverges from field studies.

Source: Mayer et al. (2024), The Cryosphere 18:2847 · Matsuoka & Murton (2008), Frost weathering · Anderson (1998), frost cracking window.
