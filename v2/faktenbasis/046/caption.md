# GeoCrimp #046 — Caption

Some rock looks wet when it's bone dry. That's serpentinite.

It starts as mantle rock — peridotite, made mostly of olivine and pyroxene. Where hot water gets into it, those minerals are converted into serpentine minerals: lizardite, antigorite, chrysotile. The rock swells, gets lighter, and ends up built from sheet-structured minerals instead of hard interlocking grains. Sheets shear easily, and serpentinite is usually thoroughly sheared. That is where the look comes from: the California Geological Survey describes structureless masses with a waxy or greasy lustre, and notes plainly that the lustre results from intense shearing of the rock.

Fun fact: keep going and you get actual soapstone. Where fluids carrying carbon dioxide react with serpentine, the products are magnesite and talc — and talc is the softest mineral on the Mohs scale. Serpentine minerals themselves are not uniformly soft, though: their hardness runs from about 3 to 6, so "soft green rock" is only half true.

The same weak sheet structure turns up in a much bigger story: serpentinite, talc and some clays are invoked as one reason certain faults creep slowly instead of locking and rupturing.

Source: Higgins (2022), CGS Note 57 — Serpentinite and Serpentine in California, California Geological Survey.
