# GeoCrimp #040 — Caption

Whether your local crag gives you ledges or big blank faces was decided before anyone climbed it.

Sedimentary rock comes in beds, and each bed acts as its own mechanical layer. When the rock is stretched, fractures open until they are far enough apart that the stress between them is relieved — and that distance scales with how thick the layer is. Thin beds crack close together and break down into ledges, small blocks and short cracks. Thick beds put their joints far apart and leave big uninterrupted faces with a few long lines. Same rock type, completely different climbing.

Fun fact: it stops at some point. Past a certain spacing-to-thickness ratio no new fractures form at all, and any further stretching just widens the cracks that are already there. Geologists call it fracture saturation.

Source: Narr & Suppe (1991), 'Joint spacing in sedimentary rocks', Journal of Structural Geology · Bai & Pollard on fracture spacing and saturation in layered rocks.
