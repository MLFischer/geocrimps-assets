# GeoCrimp #055 — Caption

Twice, ice reached the tropics and stayed there for millions of years.

The Cryogenian Period is bracketed by two of them: the Sturtian, from 717 to 659 million years ago, and the Marinoan, from about 645 to 635. The geological evidence points to ice sheets reaching low latitudes and holding — not a cold snap, an interval. The Sturtian ran for something close to sixty million years.

Getting out of it took carbon dioxide. In the standard picture, an ice-covered planet shuts down the continental weathering that normally consumes CO₂, so volcanic CO₂ accumulates in the atmosphere until the greenhouse finally overcomes the ice's albedo and the whole thing deglaciates.

Fun fact: the exit is written in the rock as a single sharp contact. Cap carbonates — limestone or dolostone up to about 200 metres thick — sit directly on top of the glacial deposits in over 50 locations on every major Neoproterozoic continent. Frozen, then hot, with nothing in between that got preserved.

Honest note: the deep-freeze framework is widely used, but the details are genuinely unsettled. A 2024 modelling paper states outright that postulated environmental conditions that created the cap carbonates lack consensus, because no single hypothesis explains their mass, their timescale and their chemistry at once.

Source: Yu et al. (2024), Three-stage formation of cap carbonates after Marinoan snowball glaciation, Nature Communications 15 · Hoffman et al. (2017), Snowball Earth climate dynamics and Cryogenian geology-geobiology, Science Advances 3:e1600983.
