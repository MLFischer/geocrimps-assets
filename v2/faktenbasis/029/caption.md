# GeoCrimp #029 — Caption

That pale stripe across the wall is a healed crack — and often the best hold on the pitch.

Quartz veins start as fractures. Hot water moving through the crust carries dissolved silica; when it cools or the pressure drops, the silica comes out of solution and crystallises on the fracture walls, growing inward until the crack is sealed shut. Do that over and over and geologists call it crack-seal. The payoff shows up much later, at the surface: quartz is one of the minerals most resistant to chemical weathering. Where a vein outlasts the rock around it, the host wears back and the vein is left standing proud — an edge, made by plumbing.

Fun fact: it doesn't always go your way. A vein can be shot through with its own fractures, and then the whole pale stripe comes off as a plate. Test it like any other hold.

Source: Bons, The formation of veins and their microstructures (structural geology review) · Goldich dissolution series — quartz at the stable end of the weathering sequence.
