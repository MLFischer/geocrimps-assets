# GeoCrimp #058 — Caption

The sandstone you are pulling on remembers how hot it once got.

Sand on its own is not rock. Burial packs the grains tighter, but packing alone does not make something you can hang off. What turns a sand into a sandstone is cement: mineral grown in the pore space between the grains. The three most common cements in terrigenous sandstones are quartz, calcite and clays.

Quartz cement is the interesting one, because it needs heat. It grows as overgrowths directly on the existing quartz grains, welding the framework together and closing the pore space. Below roughly 70 to 80 °C the reaction is simply too slow to matter — at those temperatures mechanical compaction does most of the work instead. Fluid inclusions trapped inside quartz cement in Jurassic sandstones from the Norwegian continental shelf record cementation between about 75 and 165 °C.

Which means a hard, quartz-cemented sandstone is a temperature record. It did not become solid at the surface. It became solid kilometres down, and something later brought it back up for you to climb.

Fun fact: coatings can stop the process. Clay films on the grain surfaces, or hydrocarbons filling the pores, retard or prevent quartz cementation. It is one reason oil companies care about the same rocks climbers do.

Sources: Walderhaug, Temperatures of quartz cementation in Jurassic sandstones from the Norwegian continental shelf (Journal of Sedimentary Research, 1994); Geological Digressions, The mineralogy of sandstones — matrix & cement.
