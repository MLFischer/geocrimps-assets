# GeoCrimp #053 — Caption

Rock does not only break when you overload it. It also breaks when you wait.

In the lab this is called brittle creep: rocks can deform and fail at constant applied stress, well below the strength they would show in a fast test. The mechanism is subcritical crack growth. A crack tip under tension is chemically vulnerable, and in a silica–water system the strained bridging bonds at the tip — the ones actually carrying the load — get replaced by weaker hydrogen bonds. Cracks then creep forward at stress levels that would never break the rock outright. Given long enough, a flaw grows to failure on its own.

Two things follow. Brittle creep has been shown in all major rock types, so this is not a quirk of one lithology. And the rates are extremely sensitive: stress, confining pressure, temperature and pore fluid all matter, and small changes in any of them produce order-of-magnitude changes in time-to-failure.

Honest caveat: these are laboratory results on rock samples, not on holds. They tell you a mechanism exists by which a loaded flaw grows on its own over time; they do not let anyone predict which flake goes and when. The review itself notes no current model predicts all the observed features quantitatively.

Source: Brantut, Heap, Meredith & Baud (2013), Time-dependent cracking and brittle creep in crustal rocks: A review, Journal of Structural Geology 52:17–43.
