# GeoCrimp #039 — Caption

Much of the Frankenjura isn't limestone. It's dolomite — and that is why the towers are standing.

About 150 to 160 million years ago, sponges and microbes built reef mounds in the shallow sea that covered what is now Franconia. That reef rock started out as calcite, like any limestone. Later, magnesium-rich water moved through it and replaced roughly half the calcium with magnesium. The result is dolomite: a different mineral, not merely altered limestone. And dolomite dissolves more slowly in acidic water than calcite does. So as the landscape wore down, the dolomitised reef bodies were left standing — the free-standing towers the whole area is climbed on.

Fun fact: the change is mapped in the landscape. The beds tilt, so the western valleys expose more of the bedded limestone, while towards the east it is mostly dolomite that reaches the surface.

Source: geology of the Franconian Jura (Late Jurassic reef limestone and dolomite) · carbonate dissolution kinetics — dissolution rate falls as dolomite content rises.
