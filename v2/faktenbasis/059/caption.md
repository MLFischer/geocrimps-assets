# GeoCrimp #059 — Caption

A chalk cliff is a pile of dead plankton, and the numbers are hard to believe.

Coccolithophores are single-celled algae that build themselves armour out of calcite. The individual plates are called coccoliths — minute, mostly wheel-like elements, and Cretaceous ones generally fall between 1 and 20 micrometres, with most between 4 and 12. When the alga dies the plates come apart and rain down.

Someone counted them. In 1971 Hattin and Darko cut one-centimetre cubes out of the shaly chalk of the Greenhorn Limestone in Kansas, broke them apart with ultrasound, and counted coccoliths under the microscope. Their samples held between about 5.3 × 10⁸ and 6.1 × 10⁹ coccoliths per cubic centimetre. Half a billion to six billion, in a cube the size of a sugar lump.

And here is the honest part, which is also the interesting part: those countable, intact coccoliths are not the whole rock. The authors calculated that they occupy on the order of 20 per cent or less of the volume — and called that figure tentative. The rest is broken-up coccolith debris, planktonic foraminifera, shell fragments from bivalves and oysters, fish bones, faecal pellets, terrigenous mud and pore space. Chalk is plankton, but it is mostly plankton that has already fallen apart.

Fun fact: Ehrenberg spotted coccoliths in chalk in 1836 and thought they were inorganic structures. It took until 1861, when Sorby compared them with modern deep-Atlantic mud and with whole coccospheres, for anyone to establish that they had been alive.

Source: Hattin & Darko, Technique for Determining Coccolith Abundance in Shaly Chalk of Greenhorn Limestone (Upper Cretaceous) of Kansas, Kansas Geological Survey Bulletin 202, part 2, 1971.
