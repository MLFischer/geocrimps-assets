# GeoCrimp #060 — Caption

There was never "the ice age". There were more than a hundred numbered stages, and we are living in number one.

Oxygen isotopes in the shells of deep-sea foraminifera track how much water was locked up in ice. Cesare Emiliani published the first such data in 1955 and numbered the major swings in his record. Those numbers stuck: they are the Marine Isotope Stages, and they count backwards from now.

The convention is simple. Working back from the present, which is MIS 1, even-numbered stages are high in oxygen-18 and represent cold glacial periods; odd-numbered stages are the lows, representing warm interglacials. MIS 2 is the Last Glacial Maximum. MIS 5e is the last interglacial, peaking around 123,000 years ago — the warm window before the last long cold. MIS 12 is the Anglian in Britain and the Elster in northern Europe.

And the list does not stop there. It runs past MIS 100. MIS 103 sits at the Pliocene–Pleistocene boundary, roughly 2.59 million years ago. So the boulders left behind by "the ice age" were left by one particular cycle out of dozens — and the earlier ones mostly erased each other.

Fun fact: since 2010 the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy has used one standard curve for the dates, the LR04 benthic stack. It is not a single core. It is 57 benthic oxygen-isotope records from around the world, stacked together by Lorraine Lisiecki and Maureen Raymo.

Sources: Lisiecki & Raymo, A Pliocene-Pleistocene stack of 57 globally distributed benthic δ18O records (Paleoceanography, 2005); Railsback, Marine isotope stages and substages, Fundamentals of Quaternary Science.
