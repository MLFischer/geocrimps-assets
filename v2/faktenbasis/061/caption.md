# GeoCrimp #061 — Caption

Somebody finally measured what climbing strips off an untouched boulder, and one of the findings is that the black specks left on the footholds were shoe rubber.

The experiment is unusually clean. Three previously unclimbed boulders — one sandstone, one limestone, one granite — were each climbed 500 times. The holds were photographed as a time series so the change could be tracked rather than guessed at.

The vegetation cover on the holds dropped by 0 to 15 per cent, and the first ascents were the decisive ones. Most of the damage happens the first few times a line gets climbed, not gradually over years. The loss differed by rock type: soft sandstone lost the most, because there the rock surface itself was being abraded.

Then Raman spectroscopy was pointed at the black particles sitting on the footholds. They turned out to be microplastic, abraded from climbing shoes.

Fun fact: it is not all one-way. Three years after the treatment, the microbial cover on the sandstone boulder had partly recovered.

An honest limitation, which the authors state themselves: this is one boulder per rock type. They call it preliminary insight into how vegetation cover responds in different lithologies, and that is how it should be read.

Source: Österle, Paulus, Schwietering, Audorff & Steinbauer, Mechanical impacts of climbing on cliff vegetation: Contrasting management concepts, People and Nature 8(1): 153–164, 2026 (open access).
