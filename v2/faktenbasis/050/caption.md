# GeoCrimp #050 — Caption

The Bishop Tablelands are one eruption. About six days of it.

Roughly 765,000 years ago the magma chamber under what is now Long Valley emptied out and the roof fell in. Pyroclastic flows — ash and pumice moving as a hot ground-hugging current — swept over more than 2,200 square kilometres. About 200 cubic kilometres of that landed outside the caldera; another 350 or so ponded and welded inside it. The key word is welded. The particles arrived still hot enough that the weight of the pile above pressed and fused them into solid rock. That is the difference between a drift of ash and something you can pull on.

Fun fact: the welding is not uniform. The USGS notes plainly that these deposits show various degrees of welding — so the same eruption produced rock that ranges from crumbly to solid across the plateau. And the Owens River has since cut down through the sheet, which is how the Owens River Gorge exists at all.

Source: Bishop Tuff in Long Valley Caldera, U.S. Geological Survey · Wilson & Hildreth (1997), The Bishop Tuff: New Insights from Eruptive Stratigraphy, Journal of Geology 105:407.
