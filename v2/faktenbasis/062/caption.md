# GeoCrimp #062 — Caption

No river runs straight. Not "rarely" — almost certainly never, for more than about ten times its own width.

That is Luna Leopold's line, from decades of work at the US Geological Survey, and the reason is not what most people assume. Meanders are not made by obstacles. Boulders, fallen trees and clay plugs do divert rivers, but they cannot explain the regularity, and laboratory studies show that streams meander even in ideal, highly regular material. Meandering streams have also been observed in naturally homogeneous media with no banks at all: ocean currents such as the Gulf Stream, and water channels running across the surface of a glacier.

So what makes the shape? Least work. The curve a real meander traces is a sine-generated curve — the path whose direction changes least abruptly, and therefore the curve of minimum total work in bending. Leopold and colleagues found it by asking what the most probable random walk between two fixed points looks like. Rivers are not solving a problem. They are settling into the most probable form.

And the scale is set by the river itself: meander wavelength runs at roughly 10 to 14 channel widths, the relationship fitted by Leopold and Wolman in 1960 as almost exactly linear in width. Widen the river, and the loops widen with it — a small stream and the Mississippi trace the same shape at different sizes.

Fun fact: in 1965 a derailed freight train near Greenville, South Carolina, folded its cargo of 700-foot steel rails into the same curve. The rails minimised their total bending exactly as a strip of spring steel does — and came out looking like a river.

Sources: Leopold & Langbein, River Meanders (Scientific American, 1966); Leopold & Wolman, River Meanders (GSA Bulletin 71, 1960).
