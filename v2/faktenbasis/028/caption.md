# GeoCrimp #028 — Caption

There was a time when every crag on Earth was in the same place.

From roughly 335 to 200 million years ago almost every continent was assembled into a single landmass — Pangaea — ringed by one vast ocean, Panthalassa. (Almost: the blocks that became North and South China stayed out east, across the Palaeo-Tethys.) The collision that finished the job, Laurussia against Gondwana, piled the crust up into the Central Pangean Mountains: a range that at its highest, in the early Permian, is thought to have been comparable to today's Himalaya. Then Pangaea began to come apart from about 200 million years ago, the Atlantic opened along the seam, and that one range was torn into pieces that drifted away from each other.

Fun fact: the Appalachians, the Scottish Highlands and Morocco's Anti-Atlas expose the roots of that belt — now on three continents. The High Atlas right next door is a different animal: those peaks were built by a separate, much younger collision, in the last 30 million years.

Source: Central Pangean Mountains (Caledonian to Variscan–Alleghanian collisions, Ordovician–Permian) · Anti-Atlas as the Variscan foreland of the West African Craton · High Atlas uplift 30–20 Ma (Cenozoic inversion of Mesozoic rift basins).

Which two crags would you put back next to each other if you could?
