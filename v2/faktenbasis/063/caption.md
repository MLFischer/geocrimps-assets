# GeoCrimp #063 — Caption

In September 2005 a 60-kilometre stretch of Ethiopia tore open, and most of it happened inside a week.

The Dabbahu magmatic segment sits in the Afar depression — a nascent seafloor spreading centre, still embedded in stretched continental lithosphere. Between 14 September and 4 October 2005 it produced 163 earthquakes above magnitude 3.9 and a volcanic eruption. Satellite radar caught the whole thing.

What the radar showed: the entire 60-kilometre segment ruptured, making it the largest such event to have occurred on land in the era of satellite geodesy. Elastic modelling puts the opening at up to 8 metres. Magma was injected along a dyke between 2 and 9 kilometres depth, a total intrusion volume of about 2.5 cubic kilometres.

Here is the part that changed how people think about continents breaking up. Seismic rupture accounts for only 8 per cent of the observed deformation. The faulting did not do the work — the magma did. The authors conclude that magma intrusion via dyking, rather than segmented normal faulting, maintains and probably initiated the segmentation along this sector of the Nubia–Arabia plate boundary.

Fun fact: the closest comparison is the Krafla rifting episode in Iceland, which was similar in magnitude. Krafla took ten years, from 1975 to 1984. Seismic data suggest most of the Dabbahu intrusion happened in less than a week.

Source: Wright, Ebinger, Biggs, Ayele, Yirgu, Keir & Stork, Magma-maintained rift segmentation at continental rupture in the 2005 Afar dyking episode, Nature 442: 291–294, 2006.
