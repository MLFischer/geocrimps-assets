# GeoCrimp #025 — Caption

Devils Tower is a crack pattern you can climb.

Those columns are contraction cracks. About 50 million years ago a body of phonolite magma came to rest near the top of the crust and cooled slowly. The cracks came later, after the melt had already solidified: rock that is solid keeps shrinking as it cools further, and once that pull exceeds what the rock can take, it tears itself apart along a polygonal network of joints — mostly five- and six-sided columns, some up to about 6 metres across and 180 metres long. Column width tracks cooling rate: slow cooling makes wide columns, fast cooling narrow ones. Erosion later stripped the softer rock around it away, and the joints between the columns are the cracks people climb today.

Fun fact: how the magma body was actually emplaced — laccolith, volcanic plug, or a lava coulée inside a maar-diatreme volcano — has been argued about for more than a hundred years and is still not settled.

Source: National Park Service, Devils Tower Geologic Resource Evaluation · columnar jointing as cooling-contraction fracture (Volcano World, Oregon State).
